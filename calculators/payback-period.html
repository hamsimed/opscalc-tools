Payback Period Calculator

The payback period is the time an investment takes to recover its cost from the cash it generates. It is the first screen most operations improvement projects go through. Enter the investment and annual net inflow, and optionally the asset’s useful life.

Worked example

A variable-speed drive retrofit on a fan costs 85,000 installed and saves 31,000 a year in energy. Its useful life is about 8 years.

The investment pays back within its useful life. Simple payback ignores the time value of money and cash flows after payback.

Strengths and weaknesses

Payback is easy to explain and favours fast, low-risk returns, which is useful when cash or certainty is limited. It ignores the time value of money and everything that happens after payback. A project with a slightly longer payback but a much longer life can be worth more. Use payback as a screen and net present value for the final decision.

Uneven cash flows

If savings vary year to year (ramp-up, degradation), add cumulative cash flows year by year until they exceed the investment. This calculator assumes a constant annual inflow.

Worked interpretation

In the example, 85,000 is recovered in about 2.7 years from 31,000 of annual savings. Over an 8-year life, the undiscounted net gain is 163,000. Discounting at, say, 10% a year would reduce that figure. The project still clearly pays back within its life, so payback alone is enough to pass a screening step, and a net present value calculation can follow for the final decision.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good payback period?

It depends on company policy and risk. Thresholds of 1 to 3 years are common for operational improvements, while infrastructure is often judged on longer horizons. Use your organisation’s own hurdle.
