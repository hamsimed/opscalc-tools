How to read your OEE

OEE puts three separate losses into one percentage. The single number is good for trends. For action, the factors matter more. A 65% OEE with 97% quality and 74% performance needs a different response from one with 75% availability.

Many references quote 85% as "world-class" OEE (from Seiichi Nakajima’s TPM work). Treat it as a convention, not a target. Achievable OEE depends on product mix, changeover frequency and how strictly you define planned time. Comparing a line with its own history is usually more useful than comparing it with another plant.

Common mistakes

  • Inflated ideal cycle time. Using the average or standard cycle instead of the true best cycle pushes performance toward or above 100% and hides speed losses.
  • Moving losses into "planned" time. Classifying changeovers or waiting time as planned stops raises availability on paper but hides the loss you most need to see.
  • Counting reworked parts as good. Quality should use right-first-time units. Reworked parts consumed capacity twice.
  • Averaging OEE percentages across machines. Weight by planned time, or recompute from summed times and counts.

Standards and definitions

ISO 22400-2 defines OEE as a key performance indicator for manufacturing operations management, using the same availability × effectiveness × quality structure. Write down your plant’s definitions (what counts as planned time, which stops are minor stops) and apply them the same way every shift, so trends stay meaningful.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between OEE and TEEP?

OEE measures losses against planned production time. TEEP (Total Effective Equipment Performance) measures against all calendar time, so it also shows the capacity left unused by not running nights, weekends or holidays.

Can OEE be above 100%?

No. If your calculation gives more than 100%, the ideal cycle time is usually set too slow, or counts and times come from different periods.

Should minor stops count as availability or performance loss?

Either can work if you are consistent. A common practice is to log stops above a threshold (for example 5 minutes) as downtime and let shorter stops show up as performance loss.
