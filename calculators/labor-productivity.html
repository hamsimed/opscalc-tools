    Interpreting changes

    Productivity can rise for good reasons (better methods, less rework, fewer stops) or misleading ones (product mix shifting to simpler items, deferred maintenance, hours not recorded). Check mix and quality before attributing a change to performance.

    Compare like with like: direct labour only, or all labour, but the same definition each period.

    Choosing the right output measure

    Output per labour hour works when the products are similar. With a varied mix, a simple unit count rewards making easy products. Two common fixes:

    • Standard hours earned: multiply each product’s output by its standard time, then divide by actual hours worked. The result is labour efficiency against standard.
    • Weighted units: convert products into an equivalent reference unit, using their relative work content.

    For process industries, tonnes per labour hour or tonnes per employee per year are the usual measures. They only make sense when compared at similar levels of automation.

    Linking productivity to improvement work

    Labour productivity is an outcome. The levers sit upstream: less waiting for material, shorter changeovers, fewer quality problems, better line balance. Track one or two of these drivers next to the productivity figure, so the team can see what moved it.

    Common mistakes

    • Leaving overtime hours out.
    • Comparing periods with different product mixes.
    • Counting rejected output.

    Where it’s used

    • Workforce planning.
    • Continuous improvement KPIs.
    • Benchmarking teams with similar products.

    Questions

    Should overtime hours be included?

    Yes. All hours worked should be counted, otherwise productivity looks better than it is.
