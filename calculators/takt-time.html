Takt Time Calculator

Takt time is the pace at which you must complete one unit to meet customer demand exactly. It comes from demand and available time, not from machine capability. Enter your shift pattern and daily demand, and optionally your bottleneck cycle time, to see whether the line can keep up.

Interpreting takt time

Takt time sets the rhythm for line balancing. If any station’s cycle time is above takt, that station limits output and demand cannot be met without overtime or more capacity. If every station is well below takt, the line is over-resourced for current demand.

Do not design to 100% of takt. Real lines lose time to stops, quality issues and changeovers. Many lean practitioners plan a "planned cycle time" below takt to keep a margin for these losses. The size of that margin depends on your own availability and quality history.

Takt time vs. cycle time vs. lead time

  • Takt time: the required pace, set by demand.
  • Cycle time: the actual pace at which a station or line completes units.
  • Lead time: total elapsed time for one unit to pass through the whole process, including waiting. See the Little’s Law calculator.

Common mistakes

  • Subtracting unplanned downtime from available time. Takt should reflect planned time; losses belong in OEE.
  • Using unlevelled daily orders. Takt is most useful with averaged (levelled) demand over a planning horizon.
  • Mixing units: keep demand and time in the same period (per day, per shift).

Frequently asked questions

Does takt time change when demand changes?

Yes. Takt time is recalculated whenever demand or available time changes, typically at each planning cycle. Frequent changes are a reason to level demand.

How do I estimate the number of operators needed?

A common first estimate is total manual work content per unit divided by takt time, rounded up. Then balance the work across stations so none exceeds takt.
