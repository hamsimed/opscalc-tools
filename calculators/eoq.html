    EOQ Calculator (Economic Order Quantity)

    The economic order quantity balances the cost of placing orders against the cost of holding stock. Enter annual demand, cost per order and annual holding cost per unit to get the optimal lot size and the annual costs at that quantity.

    Your inputs

    units/year

    per order

    Purchasing, receiving and invoice-handling cost of one order.

    per unit-year

    Often 15–30% of unit cost per year (capital, storage, obsolescence).

    Formula

    EOQ = √(2 × D × S ÷ H)Orders per year = D ÷ EOQTotal cost = (D ÷ Q) × S + (Q ÷ 2) × H
    Variables and units
    TermMeaningUnit
    DAnnual demandunits/year
    SFixed cost per order (admin, receiving, transport fixed part)currency/order
    HHolding cost per unit per yearcurrency/unit/year

    Worked example

    A distributor sells 12,000 units of an item a year. Each purchase order costs 75 to process, and holding one unit for a year costs 2.40.

    Inputs
    Annual demand12,000 units/year
    Cost per order75 per order
    Holding cost per unit per year2.4 per unit-year
    Results
    Economic order quantity866 units
    Orders per year13.9
    Days between orders (365-day year)26.3 days
    Annual ordering cost1,039.23
    Annual holding cost1,039.23
    Total annual cost (ordering + holding)2,078.46

    At the EOQ, ordering and holding costs are equal. The total-cost curve is flat near the optimum, so rounding to a pack or pallet size costs little.

    Using EOQ in practice

    The total-cost curve is flat around the optimum. Ordering 20% more or less than EOQ usually raises total cost by only a few percent. So round EOQ to practical pack, pallet or truck quantities without worrying.

    EOQ assumes steady demand, a fixed cost per order and no quantity discounts. With price breaks, compare total cost (including purchase price) at each break quantity.

    Estimating the holding cost

    Holding cost includes cost of capital, storage space, insurance, handling, obsolescence and shrinkage. Companies often express it as a percentage of unit cost per year. Use your own finance team’s rate where possible.

    Common mistakes

    • Including purchase price changes in the holding cost.
    • Using a cost per order that includes fixed costs that do not vary with order count.
    • Ignoring quantity discounts.

    Where it’s used

    • Purchasing lot sizes.
    • Production batch sizing.
    • Showing the value of setup reduction.

    Questions

    Is EOQ relevant for lean / just-in-time?

    Lean reduces the cost per order (setup) instead of accepting it, which mathematically lowers EOQ. The formula shows why setup reduction allows smaller lots.
