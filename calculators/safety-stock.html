    Safety Stock Calculator

    Safety stock is the buffer that protects you from stockouts when demand or lead time varies. This calculator uses the standard statistical formula. It combines demand variability and lead-time variability for a target cycle service level, and also returns the resulting reorder point.

    Formula

    SS = z × √(LT × σd² + d² × σLT²)z = Φ⁻¹(Service level)Reorder point = d × LT + SS

    TermMeaningUnit

    Worked example

    An item averages 40 units of demand per day with a daily standard deviation of 12. Lead time averages 7 days with a standard deviation of 1.5 days. The target cycle service level is 95%.

    Inputs
    Target cycle service level95 %
    Average daily demand40 units/day
    Std. deviation of daily demand12 units/day
    Average lead time7 days
    Std. deviation of lead time (optional)1.5 days
    Results
    Safety stock112 units
    Service factor (z)1.645
    Std. deviation of lead-time demand67.9 units
    Reorder point (d × LT + SS)392 units

    Assumes normally distributed, independent demand and lead time. Rounded up to whole units. For intermittent demand, this model understates the stock required.

    Choosing a service level

    Safety stock rises steeply as service level approaches 100%. Moving from 95% to 99% raises the z-factor from about 1.645 to 2.326, roughly 41% more safety stock. Set service levels by item class (for example by criticality or ABC class) instead of applying 99% everywhere.

    Cycle service level is the probability of no stockout during one replenishment cycle. It is not the same as fill rate (the share of demand served from stock). Fill rate is usually higher for the same safety stock.

    When the formula does not fit

    • Intermittent or lumpy demand (many zero-demand days) breaks the normal-distribution assumption.
    • Strong trends or seasonality need forecast-error variability, not raw demand variability.
    • For critical spares with very low usage, use a Poisson-based or risk-based method.

    Common mistakes

    • Using the standard deviation of monthly demand with lead time in days.
    • Confusing cycle service level with fill rate.
    • Applying the normal model to intermittent demand.

    Where it’s used

    • Inventory policy by ABC class.
    • Service-level versus stock trade-offs.
    • Supplier variability reviews.

    Questions

    What if lead time is constant?

    Leave the lead-time standard deviation blank (zero). The formula then becomes z × σd × √LT.
