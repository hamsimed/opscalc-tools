    Break-Even Calculator

    Break-even is the sales volume at which total contribution margin covers fixed costs: no profit, no loss. Enter fixed costs, price and variable cost per unit. Add a target profit to see the volume needed to reach it.

    Formula

    Contribution margin = Price − Variable cost per unitBreak-even units = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution marginUnits for target profit = (Fixed costs + Target profit) ÷ Contribution margin
    Variables and units
    TermMeaningUnit
    Fixed costsCosts that do not vary with volume in the period (rent, salaries, depreciation)currency
    PriceNet selling price per unitcurrency
    Variable costMaterials, energy, direct consumables per unitcurrency

    Worked example

    A small production unit has 120,000 of fixed costs per year, sells at 45 per unit and has variable costs of 27 per unit. The owners want a profit of 30,000.

    Inputs
    Fixed costs (period)120,000
    Selling price per unit45
    Variable cost per unit27
    Target profit (optional)30,000
    Results
    Break-even volume6,667 units
    Break-even revenue300,000
    Contribution margin per unit18
    Contribution margin ratio40%
    Volume for target profit8,334 units

    Volumes are rounded up to whole units.

    Using break-even analysis

    • Margin of safety = (expected volume − break-even volume) ÷ expected volume. It shows how far sales can fall before losses begin.
    • Raising price or cutting variable cost increases contribution margin and lowers break-even. Cutting fixed costs lowers it directly.
    • For several products, use a weighted average contribution margin based on the sales mix.

    Worked interpretation

    In the example, each unit contributes 18 towards fixed costs (45 − 27), a contribution margin ratio of 40%. Covering 120,000 of fixed costs takes 6,667 units, or 300,000 of revenue. Every unit sold above that adds 18 of profit, so reaching a 30,000 profit needs 1,667 more units.

    Sensitivity check: a price cut of 3 per unit lowers the margin to 15 and raises break-even to 8,000 units, 20% more volume for a 6.7% price change. Price and variable cost changes affect break-even much more strongly than they first appear.

    Common mistakes

    • Treating semi-variable costs as fully fixed.
    • Using list price instead of net realised price.
    • Ignoring capacity limits above the break-even volume.

    Where it’s used

    • Pricing decisions.
    • New product or new line evaluations.
    • Margin of safety analysis.

    Questions

    Is direct labour fixed or variable?

    It depends on how labour is managed. If headcount is fixed over the period, treat labour as fixed. If hours flex with volume (temporary staff, overtime), treat that part as variable.
