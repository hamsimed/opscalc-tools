    Belt Conveyor Capacity Calculator

    Estimate the conveying capacity of a belt conveyor in tonnes per hour. Use the load cross-section area (from troughing geometry), bulk density and belt speed. Or, if you know the load per metre of belt, use that with belt speed.

    Formula

    Volumetric capacity (m³/h) = 3,600 × A × fill × vMass capacity (t/h) = Volumetric capacity × ρMass capacity (t/h) = 3.6 × q × v

    Worked example

    An overland conveyor has a load cross-section of 0.12 m² at full trough, runs at 4.2 m/s and carries ore with a bulk density of 1.4 t/m³.

    Inputs
    Calculation methodFrom load cross-section area
    Load cross-section area0.12 m²
    Bulk density1.4 t/m³
    Fill factor100 %
    Belt speed4.2 m/s
    Results
    Conveying capacity2,540 t/h
    Capacity per 24 h60,964 t
    Volumetric capacity1,814 m³/h

    Theoretical capacity at the stated fill. Inclination, feed irregularity and material lumpiness reduce achievable capacity.

    Where the cross-section comes from

    The load area depends on belt width, troughing angle, idler arrangement, edge distance and the material’s surcharge angle. Standard methods (CEMA, ISO 5048, DIN 22101) give formulas and tables to calculate it. For inclined conveyors, apply the reduction factor from the same standard.

    Using load per metre

    Belt weighers report instantaneous t/h, and load per metre can be derived from the belt scale and speed. The relation q = Q ÷ (3.6 × v) also lets you check whether the belt is being under- or over-filled at a given throughput.

    Operational notes

    • Running consistently below design fill wastes energy per tonne. Specific energy consumption rises when belts run partly empty.
    • Overfilling causes spillage, which drives cleaning workload and idler failures.
    • In a chain of conveyors, system capacity is set by the lowest-capacity link. Check transfer points as well as belts.

    Common mistakes

    • Using full-trough area without the inclination factor.
    • Using solid density instead of loose bulk density.
    • Assuming design capacity at all feed conditions.

    Where it’s used

    • Checking capacity before increasing throughput.
    • Belt speed changes.
    • Diagnosing spillage and overfilling.

    Questions

    Why multiply by 3.6 in the load-per-metre formula?

    q (kg/m) × v (m/s) gives kg/s. Multiplying by 3,600 s/h and dividing by 1,000 kg/t gives t/h, so the factor is 3.6.
