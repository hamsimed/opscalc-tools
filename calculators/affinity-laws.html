    Fan & Pump Affinity Laws Calculator

    For centrifugal fans and pumps, flow changes with speed, pressure with speed squared and power with speed cubed. A small speed reduction therefore gives a large power reduction. This calculator applies these affinity laws to estimate the savings from a variable speed drive (VSD).

    Formula

    Flow₂ / Flow₁ = N₂ / N₁Head₂ / Head₁ = (N₂ / N₁)²Power₂ / Power₁ = (N₂ / N₁)³

    A 55 kW cooling-tower fan is slowed to 80% speed for 5,000 hours a year. Energy costs 0.12 per kWh.

    Results
    Theoretical annual savings16,104
    Power at new speed28.16 kW
    Flow at new speed80%
    Head / pressure at new speed64%
    Theoretical annual energy savings134,200 kWh

    The cube law assumes a system with no static head (friction-dominated). With static head or fixed pressure, real savings are lower. Measure power after the change to confirm.

    Why real savings are lower

    The cube law holds for systems where pressure is entirely friction (the system curve passes through zero). Most pumping systems have static head; most ventilation systems have some fixed pressure. Then the operating point moves along the system curve rather than the ideal parabola, and savings are smaller. VSD and motor losses at reduced speed also reduce the net gain. Treat the result as an upper bound and confirm with measured power.

    Where VSDs pay best

    The best candidates run long hours at partial flow controlled today by throttling valves, dampers or recirculation. Fixed-flow duties at full speed gain nothing.

    Common mistakes

    • Applying the cube law to positive-displacement pumps or compressors: it does not apply.
    • Ignoring static head in pumping systems.
    • Using nameplate power instead of measured absorbed power.

    Where it’s used

    • Screening VSD projects on fans and pumps.
    • Estimating savings from slowing over-sized fans.
    • Explaining throttling losses to non-specialists.

    Questions

    Does the cube law apply to conveyors?

    No. Belt conveyor power depends mainly on load and friction, roughly in proportion to speed and throughput, not to the cube of speed.
