    Worked example

    A 45 kW pump motor runs 6,000 hours a year at 75% load. Its efficiency is 91.0%. An IE4 replacement offers 94.6% and costs 4,200 installed. Energy costs 0.12 per kWh.

    Uses efficiency at the stated load. Part-load efficiency can differ from nameplate; use manufacturer data at your load point where available.

    Where upgrades pay

    Savings grow with running hours and load. Motors that run most of the year and are reasonably loaded are the best candidates. Lightly used or oversized motors may never pay back. Replacing at failure (instead of rewinding) usually gives the best economics, because only the price difference with a like-for-like motor counts.

    Efficiency classes

    IEC 60034-30-1 defines efficiency classes IE1 to IE4 for line-operated motors (IE5 is referenced as a future class). Class limits depend on power rating and pole count, so check the manufacturer’s data sheet for the actual efficiency at your load point.

    Look at the system first

    Motor efficiency is one link in the chain. Throttled valves, dampers and oversized pumps often waste more energy than motor losses. A variable-speed drive or correct sizing can give larger savings. Check the driven system before upgrading the motor.

    Common mistakes

    • Using nameplate efficiency at a very different load point.
    • Counting the full motor price when replacing at failure.
    • Upgrading the motor while the driven system wastes more energy.

    Where it’s used

    • Repair-or-replace decisions at motor failure.
    • Energy efficiency programmes.
    • Specifying IE classes in purchasing.

    Questions

    Does a rewound motor lose efficiency?

    It can. A poor-quality rewind can lower efficiency, while a rewind done to good-practice standards largely keeps it. Ask the repair shop for test data.
