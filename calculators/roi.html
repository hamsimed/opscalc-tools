    ROI Calculator

    Return on investment compares the net gain from an investment with its cost. Enter the total cost and total return. Add the holding period to get the annualized return, so you can compare projects of different lengths fairly.

    Your inputs

    Total value returned over the period, before subtracting the investment.

    years

    Formula

    Net gain = Total return − Investment costROI = Net gain ÷ Investment costAnnualized ROI = (Total return ÷ Investment cost)^(1 ÷ years) − 1
    Variables and units
    TermMeaningUnit
    Investment costAll costs to acquire and implementcurrency
    Total returnTotal benefits received over the period (savings, extra margin, resale value)currency
    YearsHolding or evaluation periodyears

    Worked example

    An automation project costs 50,000 and is expected to return 68,000 in labour and scrap savings over 3 years.

    Inputs
    Total investment cost50,000
    Total return (gross benefits)68,000
    Holding period (optional)3 years
    Results
    Return on investment36%
    Net gain18,000
    Annualized ROI (CAGR)10.79%

    Simple ROI ignores when cash arrives. For multi-year projects, also check payback and NPV.

    Limitations of simple ROI

    Simple ROI ignores when the returns arrive and how uncertain they are. Two projects with the same ROI can differ a lot if one pays back in year one and the other in year five. For capital decisions, also look at payback period, net present value and risk.

    Building a credible ROI for an improvement project

    • Count only savings you can measure: reduced hours, scrap, energy (see energy cost) or downtime (see downtime cost).
    • Include implementation costs: training, lost production during installation, maintenance.
    • State the baseline and how savings will be verified after the project.

    Worked interpretation

    In the example, the project returns 68,000 on 50,000, a net gain of 18,000 and an ROI of 36% over three years. Annualised, that is about 10.8% per year: a better figure for comparing with a 1-year project or with the company’s cost of capital. A 36% ROI that takes three years is not the same as 36% in one year.

    Common mistakes

    • Using gross benefits as net gain.
    • Ignoring implementation and running costs.
    • Comparing projects of different lengths without annualising.

    Where it’s used

    • Project screening.
    • Post-implementation reviews.
    • Comparing improvement ideas.

    Questions

    Can ROI be negative?

    Yes. If total return is less than cost, net gain and ROI are negative: the investment lost money.
